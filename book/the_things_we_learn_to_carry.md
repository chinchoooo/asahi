# The Things We Learn to Carry
*A disguised memoir*

## Prologue: A Boy Who Thought the World Was Small

Once, there was a boy who thought the world was a fairly simple place.

He was born in 2007, into a family that had already imagined quite a lot of things for him. He did not know that, of course. At that age, the world was mostly made up of schoolbooks, examination results, holidays, karate classes, and the familiar faces of the people who loved him.

His father moved to another country when he was five. The distance did not mean that his father disappeared from his life. He remained involved, sometimes more involved than the boy knew how to understand. There were conversations, expectations, instructions, and decisions that travelled across the distance between them. But the boy was young, and when his father came home, he was simply happy to see him.

His childhood had a rhythm.

His mother and grandmother would sit beside him for hours, teaching him Hindi and practically every other subject in his school curriculum. There was always another chapter, another exercise, another examination to prepare for. His holidays were rarely entirely his own. Much of his childhood revolved around marks, and he was good at collecting them.

He was a topper until the eighth grade.

He was also a child who cried when he was unprepared. Once, during an oral examination for a Hindi qualification, he sat before the examiner without having studied sufficiently. He became overwhelmed and started crying. The examiner felt sorry for him and passed him.

It was a small incident, but perhaps it belonged to an earlier, gentler version of his life, when a child could cry in front of an adult and sometimes the adult would simply let him be.

There was karate, too.

Every time he received a new belt or moved up a rank, there was something to celebrate. After class, he and his father would sometimes go to McDonald's. A new belt meant food, conversation, and the simple happiness of having achieved something.

He did not need much.

He was happy when his mother taught him. He was happy when his grandmother helped him prepare for examinations. He was happy when his father returned. He was happy when he earned another belt.

He did not yet understand that happiness could become something people expected him to justify.

He did not know that one day he would look back at those years and wonder when exactly life had stopped feeling so uncomplicated.

For the first twelve or thirteen years, he was simply a boy growing up.

Then the world changed.

## Chapter I: The Year the Telephone Became Frightening

He was in the eighth grade when the pandemic arrived.

There are certain periods of history that can be explained through dates, statistics, and the names of cities. But the boy would remember those years differently.

He would remember the telephone.

Every call seemed to bring news of another death. Every conversation carried the possibility that someone familiar had become another name in a message, another photograph, another announcement that someone had passed away.

People called one another to ask for news. They called to share news. Sometimes they called because they had nothing left to do except hope someone would pick up.

The boy was twelve or thirteen. He should have been worrying about school, examinations, friends, and the ordinary inconveniences of adolescence.

Instead, he was learning that people could disappear.

One evening, at around nine, he spoke to his grandfather.

It was an ordinary conversation. There was nothing about it that suggested it would become important. Nobody knew that they were approaching a final goodbye. Nobody stopped to memorise the words.

By the next morning, his grandfather was dead.

And there was a photograph.

He still remembered the photograph of his grandfather inside an icebox.

Some images do not become memories in the usual sense. They become something more permanent, something the mind refuses to put away. Years can pass, circumstances can change, and the rest of life can become busy, but that image remains remarkably clear.

During those years, something else happened.

For almost two years, his memory seemed to disappear. He would later say that he remembered absolutely nothing from that period. Not merely that the years were difficult or that he had forgotten some events. It felt as though an entire section of his life had gone missing.

By 2022, he began coming back into motion.

He returned to academics, worked through his school examinations, and began taking an active role in his own education again. His Term 2 board results were good. He brought his mother into the process, gradually rebuilt his routine, and tried to recover the person he had been before everything became unfamiliar.

But the pandemic had changed the way he understood the world.

Before it, death had been something that happened somewhere else, to people he did not know.

After it, death was a conversation at nine in the evening.

He had learned something at thirteen that some people manage to avoid learning for most of their lives: an ordinary day does not promise another ordinary day.

And he never quite forgot it.

## Chapter II: The Boy Everyone Had Already Decided to Become

Around fourteen or fifteen, he began understanding that his family had expectations that extended far beyond academic performance.

He grew up in a deeply orthodox household. There were inherited ideas about religion, tradition, social conduct, and what a respectable young man ought to become.

He was told not to talk to girls. He was told to keep away from them, that such distractions would affect his future, and that he had responsibilities towards his ancestors that he should not forget.

He was still young enough to be forming his own opinions, but old enough to realise that some opinions were not particularly welcome.

The expectations were rarely presented as possibilities. They were presented as instructions.

By then, he had already begun understanding how much of his life had been organised around doing things correctly.

Study well. Score well. Behave properly. Do not disappoint anyone.

In the eleventh grade, he made a private decision.

He would study on his own. He would not make his mother suffer because of his academic responsibilities. She had already done so much for him, and he wanted to take charge of his education without requiring her to spend every evening sitting beside him.

He did.

He studied, prepared, and eventually completed his school examinations.

But there was one thing that would stay with him long after the examinations ended.

His father had once questioned whether he was particularly intelligent. After an unsuccessful performance in an important entrance examination, he had said something along the lines of not believing that the boy was especially smart and doubting whether he would score well in his board examinations either.

The boy remembered those words.

He was sixteen when the board results arrived.

Ninety-six point four per cent.

There was a particular kind of silence that followed that result. The earlier doubts suddenly had less room to stand. His father, who had been so certain about what the boy could not achieve, was now considerably more pleased with what he had achieved.

But something about the experience remained.

He had wanted to be believed before the result, not merely congratulated after it.

He had wanted someone to look at him and say that he could do it, without requiring him to produce a number first.

It was a small distinction, perhaps.

But some distinctions follow people for years.

## Chapter III: The University That Was Chosen for Him

At seventeen, he faced a decision that would change the way he understood independence.

He had received admission offers from several engineering institutions. There were universities he genuinely wanted to attend, including institutions that represented the possibility of a different life.

There was the possibility of leaving home.

For nearly a year, he begged his mother to let him go.

He did not want to run away from education. He wanted to experience it differently. He wanted to study somewhere he had chosen, meet different people, live independently, and perhaps discover who he was outside the small circle of expectations that had followed him throughout childhood.

He had been admitted to several places. He had worked for those opportunities. He had options.

But his parents were afraid.

They wanted him in a government institution. They believed it was the safer decision, the more reliable decision, perhaps the decision that carried the least uncertainty.

His father was actively involved in the process. He was not a distant observer who had no influence over what happened. He participated in the decision, expressed his preferences, and made his position very clear.

But the way those preferences were communicated mattered.

The boy was made to feel that if he did not choose the institution his father wanted, he would be placing his entire future at risk.

There were words to the effect of:

*If you don't go here, I don't know what will happen with your life, and I'm not responsible for it.*

It was a particularly difficult position for a seventeen-year-old.

He was being told what to choose, while simultaneously being made to feel that the consequences of choosing differently would be entirely his responsibility.

It was not a straightforward disagreement about universities. It was a decision wrapped in fear, guilt, and the weight of parental authority.

He wanted his mother to understand him. He wanted her to let him leave.

He kept asking.

Eventually, the decision was made for him.

And the irony was difficult to ignore.

He had worked hard enough to get into several institutions. He had earned the opportunity to choose between them. But the very people who had encouraged him to study, compete, and perform had decided that the final choice would not belong to him.

The first two semesters were miserable.

He hated the college. He hated the circumstances that had brought him there. He hated the feeling of waking up every day knowing that he was living through a decision he had spent nearly a year trying to avoid.

He would take long periods of leave, sometimes almost a month in a semester, and then return to college and attend regularly.

Eventually, it became part of his identity there.

People knew the pattern. He would disappear for a while, then return, attend classes, and carry on as though nothing particularly unusual had happened.

But there was a reason.

He did not want to be there.

He began associating the institution with something far larger than lectures, examinations, or placement statistics. It became a reminder of a time when he had asked for something repeatedly and had not been heard.

He would later describe it as a kind of trauma.

And perhaps the most frustrating part was that he could not simply leave.

He needed to finish the degree. He needed a job. He needed financial independence. He needed to build an exit that would not require him to ask anyone for permission.

So he stayed.

Not because he had made peace with the decision.

Because he had not yet built the means to make another one.

## Chapter IV: The Woman Who Used to Sit Beside Him

While all of this was happening, his mother was going through a difficult period of her own.

For approximately two years, from 2022 to 2024, she had worked extraordinarily long hours. There were stretches when work seemed to consume almost the entirety of her day. She was trying to provide, trying to build security, trying to make sure that the future would be a little less uncertain.

Then she stopped working.

Her health deteriorated.

At seventeen, the boy watched the woman who had once sat beside him for hours teaching him school subjects struggle with the ordinary movements of daily life.

For a long period, she could barely move around the house. She could still walk, but slowly. Leaving her room became difficult. The pace of the household changed with her health.

She was living with a serious muscular condition, and the consequences were felt throughout the family.

His father spoke about the family's retirement plans and the implications of her leaving work. Sometimes he spoke as though her suffering had somehow become a financial inconvenience.

On one occasion, he suggested that perhaps her suffering was connected to the family not praying to God.

The boy could not tolerate that.

He had spent years being told to behave, respect his elders, follow instructions, and understand that his parents knew better.

But when his mother was struggling, something in him gave way.

For the first time in his life, he shouted at his father.

He told him off. He refused to remain silent while his mother was spoken about that way.

His mother cried.

He remembered it because it was the first time he had broken through the fear that had kept him quiet for so long.

But there was no victory in it.

He was still seventeen. He was still studying. His college problems had not disappeared. His mother's health had not suddenly improved. His family was still his family the following morning.

He had simply discovered that there were things he could no longer watch without saying something.

His mother had once been his safest person.

She had taught him, supported him, helped him through examinations, and spent countless hours making sure that he was prepared for the world.

Now he was watching her struggle with a world that had become particularly unkind to her.

He did not know what to do with that reversal.

## Chapter V: The Forty-Six Days

There came a point when he could no longer maintain the appearance that everything was fine.

He had continued studying, attending classes when he could, managing responsibilities, and trying to become the person everyone needed him to be.

But the accumulated weight of everything had become difficult to carry.

At eighteen or nineteen, he experienced a period of withdrawal that lasted forty-six days.

His birthday happened during that episode.

For forty-six days, he remained at home. He barely bathed. He did very little. He ate junk food, stayed in his room, and watched the days pass without much distinction between one and the next.

The boy who had once organised his holidays around examination preparation could barely organise an ordinary day.

Eventually, he broke down.

He cried, and his mother realised that things had become serious.

He received a diagnosis of depression and anxiety.

Treatment began.

He would never describe that period as a particularly graceful transformation. There was no sudden moment when he understood everything or woke up one morning feeling entirely different.

There was simply a recognition that something had happened and that he needed help.

His mother became involved again.

She spoke to his college department and his Head of Department. She helped him return to classes, helped explain the circumstances, and made sure he could continue his education.

He would remember this with enormous gratitude.

There was also something else she did that mattered immensely to him: she protected his privacy.

She did not turn his diagnosis into a public announcement. She told the people who needed to know, and he spoke to a couple of friends himself.

For a boy who had spent years feeling that everything about him was being evaluated, having something so personal remain private was an extraordinary kindness.

His mother had given him that.

He would not forget it.

He returned to college and began attending regularly. He got his attendance in order. He completed what was required.

From a distance, it might have looked like he had recovered.

But recovery was not a straight line.

He could attend college and still dread entering the building. He could complete an assignment and still feel disconnected from the reason he had started. He could laugh with his mother and still have no idea how to tell her that he was struggling.

He had learned to function again.

He was still trying to learn how to live.

## Chapter VI: The People in the Same House

His relationship with his mother slowly became something he did not quite recognise.

There was a time when he could tell her nearly everything.

He could complain about school. He could talk about what had upset him. He could disagree with her and expect that the conversation would eventually find its way back to normal.

But as her health and emotional circumstances became more difficult, she sometimes felt that hearing his complaints was hurting her.

And the boy decided that he would stop.

He would not add to her burden.

He would not tell her every little thing that went wrong. He would not bring every disappointment home. He would smile, make jokes, play around, ask about her day, and try to keep the atmosphere lively.

He thought that at least one of them should be happy.

That became one of the quietest decisions of his life.

He stopped talking about many of the things that mattered to him, not because they had stopped hurting, but because he did not want his mother to hurt alongside him.

He still loved her.

He was still grateful for her.

He still remembered the woman who had taught him every subject and helped him through childhood.

But the relationship changed.

They began behaving almost like roommates.

They shared a home, ordinary conversations, small jokes, and the details of everyday life. But he no longer knew how to bring her the parts of himself that were difficult.

He did not know how to tell her that he was frightened of losing her.

Because he was.

During the pandemic, he had learned how suddenly people could disappear. Now his mother was living with a condition that made the possibility of losing her particularly frightening.

Whenever she mentioned her own death, even casually, his eyes filled with tears.

He could not always explain why.

It was as though his mind had already seen this particular ending and was desperate not to experience it again.

He had spoken to someone at nine in the evening once.

The next morning, that person was gone.

He did not want another ordinary conversation to become a final one.

And so he became careful.

Perhaps too careful.

There were things he wanted to say but did not. There were disagreements he avoided. There were conversations he never started because he could not bear the possibility that they might affect her health.

He loved his mother, but he missed being able to talk to her.

He missed the safe place she had once been.

And he did not know how to get it back.

## Chapter VII: The Grandmother and the Boy She Remembered

His grandmother had helped raise him.

She had taught him, sat beside him, and watched him grow into the child everyone knew as disciplined and academically successful.

For a long time, he thought that relationship would remain one of the constants of his life.

In 2023, there was a sentence she said to him before he left after a visit.

She told him that even if he wanted to fight with his mother, he should not. She did not know how much longer she would be around.

He remembered those words.

It is strange how certain sentences remain with us, particularly when they are spoken by people we are afraid of losing.

But their relationship became complicated.

During his difficult period, she criticised him. She continued to compare him with the boy she remembered, the one who was disciplined, the one who scored perfect marks, the one who was expected to know how to behave.

She had helped raise him into that boy, and perhaps she struggled to recognise the person who had emerged after everything that happened.

He felt hurt.

He also felt guilty for being hurt.

There was gratitude for everything she had done and resentment for the way she treated him now. There was affection for the grandmother who had taught him and frustration towards the person who seemed unable to accept that he had changed.

They sometimes went weeks without speaking, despite living in the same house.

He did not always want to restart the conversation.

He was afraid that another conflict might worsen her health. He had already seen how family conflict could affect people he loved, and he did not want to become the reason for another decline.

So he remained quiet.

He was beginning to discover that silence could preserve a relationship and damage it at the same time.

## Chapter VIII: The Rock

There was a time when the boy cried whenever he was sad.

Now he described himself as a rock.

He could talk about things that had hurt him for years and then return to normal within minutes. Sometimes he would cry while explaining something and, almost immediately afterwards, feel as though the emotion had disappeared.

He had become very good at containing things.

He could smile in front of his mother. He could joke with people at college. He could appear perfectly ordinary when someone asked how he was doing.

But he often felt as though he had become a closed book, a closed rock.

He once compared himself to a blade of Damascus steel, folded, hardened, and etched in acid into something he had never intended to become.

It was an image that made sense to him.

He had been shaped by circumstances, expectations, disappointments, and the necessity of enduring things he did not know how to change.

He had learned to anticipate the worst outcome.

His thoughts ran at the speed of a supercomputer, calculating possibilities, failures, and every way an ordinary situation might go wrong.

He had become extremely alert to disappointment.

Even when something good happened, he struggled to remain with the feeling.

He could achieve something substantial, acknowledge it for a moment, and move directly to the next task.

An opportunity arrived.

Okay, that's done. What's next?

A difficult semester ended.

Okay, that's done. What's next?

A research project progressed. Another application was submitted. Another responsibility was completed.

He kept moving.

But he wondered whether he had stopped enjoying things along the way.

There were two school friends with whom he still felt particularly comfortable. Beyond that, he often worried that people became annoyed with him. He learned to smile, make jokes, and behave as though everything was fine.

Even jokes about his appearance and weight began affecting him more than he wanted to admit.

He had become accustomed to being the person who made things easier for everyone else.

He was less certain how to be the person who needed something.

There were moments when anger broke through the numbness, too.

One particularly difficult day, after missing his prescribed medication, a serious trigger pushed him into an overwhelming episode. He began throwing things around.

The trigger had been significant, but the intensity of his response frightened him.

It made him realise that being quiet did not necessarily mean that everything inside him had become peaceful.

He continued treatment. The familiar drowsiness he had once experienced after taking his medication eventually became less noticeable. He sometimes wondered whether the medicine was working as it should, or whether he had simply become better at pushing everything down and continuing with his life.

He knew he needed help.

He also knew that finding someone with whom he felt sufficiently safe to discuss everything would take time.

He had become a person who could describe his suffering more easily than he could actually share it.

## Chapter IX: The Future He Was Trying to Build

Through all of this, he had not stopped wanting a future.

In fact, perhaps he wanted one more desperately than ever.

He studied, built projects, explored research, pursued internships, and tried to develop skills that existed outside the boundaries of his college curriculum.

He was interested in technology, research, and the possibility of building things that were genuinely useful.

He wanted to be judged by what he could do.

Not merely by the marks on a transcript.

Not by how regularly he attended an institution he had never wanted to enter.

Not by how closely he resembled someone's idea of a perfect boy.

He wanted to be competent, independent, and capable of supporting himself.

Most importantly, he wanted to leave home.

Not necessarily to escape every person in it.

He still loved his mother. He was grateful for his grandmother's place in his childhood. His relationship with his father was complicated, but his father remained an important influence in his life.

He simply wanted some distance.

He wanted a place where he could make decisions without having to account for every possible disappointment they might cause.

He wanted to wake up in the morning and decide how the day would go.

He wanted to speak to people without calculating whether his words might make somebody unwell.

He wanted to disagree without feeling responsible for the consequences of every disagreement.

He wanted to have a life that belonged to him.

But leaving required money.

Money required a job.

A job required completing his degree.

And completing his degree required continuing in a place that still reminded him of the decisions he had not been allowed to make.

So he continued.

He attended classes. He maintained his attendance. He worked on his projects. He searched for opportunities.

He tried to build a future one small piece at a time.

There was something almost cruel about the arrangement.

He had to remain in a place that made him unhappy in order to acquire the means to leave the circumstances that made him unhappy.

He was rowing towards freedom, but the oars were made of the very responsibilities he was trying to escape.

## Chapter X: At Least One of Us

Perhaps the most revealing sentence he ever said was not about college, his father, his grandmother, or the pandemic.

It was a sentence about his mother.

At least one of us should be happy.

He had said it because he felt that sharing his problems was hurting her.

But somewhere along the way, the sentence had become a philosophy.

He would make sure his mother was happy, even if he was not.

He would try not to upset his grandmother, even if he was hurt.

He would satisfy the expectations placed on him, even if he did not agree with them.

He would remain in college, even if he hated it.

He would finish his degree, get a job, and leave only when he could do so on his own merit.

He would smile in front of people, even when he had nothing to smile about.

He would carry the unpleasant parts quietly.

And whenever someone asked him how he was doing, he could give them a version of the answer that would not require a difficult conversation.

But there was a problem with that arrangement.

He was also a person.

He had his own disappointments, ambitions, fears, and grievances. He had wanted things that did not happen. He had been frightened. He had been angry. He had been hurt by people he loved.

He had spent years trying to ensure that the people around him were not burdened by his feelings.

But where were those feelings supposed to go?

Perhaps they went into the long absences from college.

Perhaps they went into the forty-six days.

Perhaps they went into the sudden anger, the sleepless nights, the tears that arrived whenever his mother mentioned death.

Perhaps they went into the silence between him and his grandmother.

Or perhaps they simply accumulated, one ordinary day at a time, until even he could no longer tell how much he was carrying.

He did not need to hate his family to admit that some things had hurt him.

He did not need to stop loving his mother to admit that their relationship had changed.

He did not need to deny everything his grandmother had done for him to acknowledge that her words sometimes wounded him.

He did not need to turn his father into a stranger to understand that his decisions and expectations had affected him deeply.

Love and hurt had become occupants of the same house.

Neither had agreed to leave.

## Epilogue: The Shore

At night, when the house became quieter, he sometimes imagined himself rowing.

There was darkness around him and a road through the landscape of his own mind that he used as an oar.

Sadness was behind him.

Something treacherous lay ahead.

He was trying to cross a bridge, trying to cross a road, trying to reach a place where he could finally stand on his own.

He did not know exactly what he was looking for.

Maybe it was a small apartment in another city.

Maybe it was a job that paid enough for him to support himself.

Maybe it was a room where he could spend an entire evening without explaining his decisions.

Maybe it was simply a morning when he woke up and felt that the day belonged to him.

He was not looking to become a famous person or an extraordinary success.

He did not need another examination result to prove that he was capable.

He had already spent enough of his life proving things.

What he wanted now was something much less impressive to the outside world.

He wanted to enjoy a meal without having earned it.

He wanted to pursue something because it interested him.

He wanted to laugh without checking whether he was making too much noise.

He wanted to be sad without worrying about who might become sad because of him.

He wanted to have someone around whom he did not need to perform.

He wanted to be able to say that something hurt without immediately explaining why he had no right to complain.

And perhaps, most of all, he wanted to meet the person he might have become if he had not spent so much of his life trying to become what everyone else needed.

Sometimes he remembered the little boy from before the pandemic.

The boy who came home with a new karate belt.

The boy whose mother and grandmother sat beside him for hours.

The boy who was excited when his father visited.

The boy who cried during an examination because he had not prepared.

The boy who thought the world would be kind if he simply did everything correctly.

He did not know whether that boy still existed.

Perhaps he was buried beneath years of memories, expectations, disappointment, and fear. Perhaps he had grown older and changed beyond recognition.

But sometimes, in the middle of an ordinary day, the older boy could still recognise something familiar.

A small excitement about a project.

A new idea.

A good conversation with one of his friends.

The possibility of going somewhere new.

A future that might not look anything like the one his family had imagined.

Those moments did not last very long.

But they were there.

He had once thought that his life would be measured by the marks he received, the institutions he entered, and the expectations he fulfilled.

He was slowly discovering that none of those things could answer the question he had been carrying for years.

Who was he, when nobody needed anything from him?

He did not have an answer.

Not yet.

The boat continued moving.

The boy did not know how far the shore was, and there were nights when he could not see it at all.

But he kept rowing.

Not because he had become invincible.

Not because he had finally understood everything.

Not because the past had stopped hurting.

He kept rowing because somewhere, beyond the darkness, there might be a life in which he no longer had to explain why he deserved to be there.

A life that was not a repayment.

A life that was not an obligation.

A life that was his.

And for the first time in a long while, he allowed himself to imagine that he might reach it without having to become somebody else first.

He did not know what he was.

He did not know where he was.

He only knew that he was still rowing.

Still searching.

Still here.
